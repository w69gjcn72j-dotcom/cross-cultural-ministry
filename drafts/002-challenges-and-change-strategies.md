Challenges and Change Strategies for a Multicultural Immigrant Church

Summary: Four challenges facing a bilingual immigrant congregation trying to disciple three generations and their neighbours — and five things I would do about them. The overview essay; the pieces that follow take up single strands at greater length.

What follows came out of a course on the challenges and opportunities facing the immigrant church, taught by Dr Thomas Lee and Senior Pastor Greg Haroutunian. Many challenges were raised over those four days. These are the four I found most directly applicable where I serve: as the senior minister of a multi-ethnic immigrant Anglican church in a highly diverse community.

## One — Cultural intelligence

The first is the simplest to name and the hardest to acquire.

Michael Chu's *Intercultural Competence* observes that there are many "unexpected disputes and setbacks" in immigrant ministry in Sydney (Chu 2019, xx), and argues that a good number of them become intelligible once you look at them through the lens of cultural intelligence. Chu works with four factors: metacognitive CQ, the capacity for conscious intercultural awareness; cognitive CQ, actual knowledge of another culture's norms and conventions; motivational CQ, the drive to direct real attention and energy towards functioning in a different culture; and behavioural CQ, the capacity to act appropriately, in word and in body, when you are actually in the room (Chu 2019, 143–144).

In a city where 37.4% of residents speak a language other than English at home, it is not optional for ministers to reach an adequate level of cultural intelligence — not merely to know and love those from another culture inside their own churches, but those outside them.

I have taken this up at length in a separate piece, [Intercultural Competence](003-intercultural-competence.html).

## Two — A heightened sensitivity to conflict

A theme that surfaced again and again across the lectures and discussions: immigrant churches feel intergenerational and intercultural conflict more keenly than other churches do.

Intergenerational conflict is the difference of opinion, posture and approach between one generation and another. Intercultural conflict is the difference between one readily identifiable culture and another. Both occur in every church. What is distinctive about the immigrant church is that the two so often coincide. One generation is identified with one culture — first-generation, Mandarin-speaking, Chinese. The next defines itself by another — second-generation, English-speaking, Western. When the fault lines run together, every disagreement about music or money or timing is also, underneath, a disagreement about who we are. Sadly, these differences often end in conflicts that damage both the relationships between members and our witness to the world.

But the reading and the discussion both pressed a caution on us: be careful not to sort everyone into one camp or the other. There is a cultural and a generational *continuum* among our leaders and our members, and no single position on it supersedes the rest. As Paul reminds us, "For we were all baptized by one Spirit into one body — whether Jews or Greeks, whether slaves or free — and we were all given one Spirit to drink. Indeed, the body is not one part but many" (1 Corinthians 12:13–14). God has made each of us, in his perfect timing and providence, to be who we are, culturally and generationally. We must love and respect those whom God loves and has saved in Christ, knowing he has made them according to his purpose, just as he has made us. In his wisdom he has made us to reach the world in culturally and generationally particular ways.

One image from the course has stayed with me. Dr Lee presented Ken Fong's model of acculturation across immigrant generations as three bodies of water.

[figure: figures/acculturation-waters.svg | The three waters. First-generation immigrants are the rivers of dreamers — like bass, they swim freely within the bounds of their own ethnicity. The bicultural generation are the bays of transition — like salmon, they move between fresh water and salt. The majority culture is the sea of inevitability — like cod, they swim freely in the wider community. After Ken Fong's model of acculturation over immigrant generations, as presented by Dr Thomas Lee.]

The question the model puts to a church is a practical one: are we fishing from a stationary dock, or floating downstream in a boat? The first works from the first generation to the second. The second is what is needed from the second generation onwards. Naming which one we are doing has helped me appreciate the difference between our people rather than be exasperated by it.

## Three — Diversity in a church that looks homogeneous

In an online session, Pastor Enoch Liao opened by asking whether we thought of our churches as diverse. Most of us from ethnic-specific ministries said no — we look alike. We had been measuring diversity by ethnicity, and by that measure we failed.

He then named something painful and true. In the United States, in Australia, and in many other places, ethnic churches are widely seen as places of conflict, for exactly the intercultural and intergenerational reasons above. Members — and, if we are honest, ministers — can be embarrassed by their own church. I have watched this through my whole ministry, particularly among second-generation and even first-generation immigrants who experienced those conflicts first-hand and have no intention of going back.

Then he turned it over. The presence of conflict shows there are differences of opinion, and differences of opinion are what diversity actually *is*. A church that looks ethnically diverse may be culturally homogeneous — everyone lives in the city, eats the same food, holds the same political and cultural views. Meanwhile a church that looks uniform to a casual observer may contain enormous difference. One of my classmates leads a congregation of people almost all from South America. They may look similar. They speak different languages and come from very different worlds.

The diversity inside ethnic churches ought to be an attraction to Christian ministry, not a repellent — even when it ends in conflict. Because our Lord Jesus taught us to love those who are not like us. It was Jesus who said, "For if you love those who love you, what reward will you have? Don't even the tax collectors do the same?" (Matthew 5:46). If we only love those who are culturally the same as we are, are we not simply loving ourselves?

One of the most powerful testimonies the church has is the way we love one another as Christ loves us. Would it not be a striking thing if ethnic churches, with all our cultural differences, became known as places of forgiveness and reconciliation rather than places of division?

Pastor Greg Haroutunian gave us one. As an Armenian pastor of an Armenian church, he was able to partner with a Turkish pastor towards reconciliation between the two peoples. The Turkish pastor went with a group of Armenian Christians to apologise for the Armenian genocide carried out by his ancestors. It was a vivid reminder of the love of Christ, which breaks every divide it meets.

## Four — The first generation is not the first generation any more

Pastor John Oh raised the question of the immigrant church's identity by telling his own story. He came as a young boy. His parents could not speak English, and the Korean church they attended helped the family enormously as they made a life in America.

Forty years later he welcomed a new Korean family into the church — and they did not need any of it. They had arrived already prepared, having gathered everything they needed online before leaving Korea. The Korean church of the last century was known for helping Korean immigrants settle into America and bringing them into the church. The Korean church of this century is simply not as necessary to new Korean arrivals. Attendance at Korean churches in the United States dropped from around 900,000 in the early 2000s to approximately 470,000 in a little over twenty years.

His conclusion was that we should stop thinking of ourselves as *immigrant* churches and start thinking of ourselves as *diaspora* churches — proactively engaging an immigrant community that keeps changing, rather than serving as a pocket of one remembered culture.

This lands hard in Sydney. Many of our ethnic churches are culturally distinct not only from the community around them but from the contemporary culture of their own home country. The culture we remember is almost certainly not the culture the new arrivals are leaving. We are, without noticing, curating a museum and calling it home.

May our ethnic-specific churches seek to engage well not only with people of a similar culture in their own generation, but with the wider diaspora.

## What I would do about it

Five steps, if I were given the opportunity to lead our church to be more effective in bringing the gospel to those around us.

### A double helix of discipleship and mentoring

Dr Lee presented a double-helix model of discipleship and academia. Christ has commanded us to make disciples of all nations — people from our own culture and from every other. And, as we saw across the course, a great deal of conflict in the immigrant church comes down to a lack of cultural intelligence and a lack of love for those who differ from us. We need good, Christ-honouring, other-person-centred disciple-makers who can form others into the likeness of Christ. Without discipleship, without teaching the commands of Christ to the next generation, we will not be the church Christ has called us to be.

At the same time we must work at engaging the world well. Many people have thought hard about the questions we face and have done serious research on them. We do well to learn from them and engage their ideas, even where those ideas do not fully align with our own.

I would adapt the model slightly: a double helix of discipleship and *mentorship*. I would love to set up an academy researching the latest thinking on immigrant churches, but that is beyond my capacity. What I can do is walk beside a fellow believer, disciple them in the word, mentor them in ministry skill, and point them to where the knowledge is. I am currently preparing to be a ministry supervisor, and my hope is to help fellow ministers grow in their knowledge and love of Christ even as they disciple those in their own care.

### The house

Pastor Greg presented a two-storey house with the entrance on the lower level. Downstairs is where we welcome people, help them understand and believe the gospel, and bring them into the church. As they grow through the word and prayer, and through training, we are helping them up the stairs. Upstairs is where leaders are formed — so that they will come back down and serve on the ground floor.

It is a vivid picture of what we need. Most people only ever see the lower level. Others assume they belong on the upper one. Very few see the staircase in between — the training required before taking a seat of responsibility. Worse, some who have reached the upper level refuse to come back down and serve. I would like to introduce this picture to the church so that we can see the shape of the whole building we are living in.

### A God-dream on four horizons

Will Mancini's *God Dreams* was strongly commended to us, and Dr Lee generously shared how his own church is working through it. It is a genuinely useful book for thinking long-term, and for helping "people go to places where they are given a larger story to live in" (Mancini 2016, 16–17).

I have written about this separately, in [God Dreams: Vision on Four Horizons](004-god-dreams.html).

### A G7 organisation

In one of the course presentations, Bolman and Deal's (4+1) framework was introduced: it takes the biblical teaching of the church as the body of Christ and uses body *systems*, rather than body *parts*, to describe the different functions of a church. I had been working on something similar, with seven systems rather than five. I call it the G7.

| System | Body system | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| Glorify | Circulatory | Active engagement between our Lord and his people, in prayerful obedience |
| Grow | Nervous | Effective tools for groups: discipling, teaching, rebuking, correcting, training |
| Go | Respiratory | Joyfully pointing people to Jesus — bringing them in, and sending them out |
| Giftedness | Endocrine | A clear leadership and followership pipeline; mentors, coaches, trainers |
| Gregarious | Integumentary | Care for those in need; leaving no one unloved at church |
| Governance | *provisionally* nervous | Oversight and decision-making |
| Glue | *provisionally* muscular | Administration and maintenance |

The last two are not settled. Systems thinking is the point: we want to think in systems so that we do not unintentionally damage one part of the body of Christ while lavishing attention on another.

### All-together and parallel services, both

The course left me more convinced, not less, of the importance of *both* multi-ethnic churches — which may in fact be culturally homogeneous — and ethnic-specific churches — which, though monoethnic, may be more genuinely diverse than the multi-ethnic ones.

Gary McIntosh and Alan McMahan's *Being the Church in a Multi-Ethnic Community* was very helpful in affirming both. The multi-ethnic model is a good model; so is the monocultural one. The principle for both is that neither may be exclusive of the other. Room must be made for both. I have taken this up at length in [Being the Church in a Multi-Ethnic Community](005-being-the-church-multi-ethnic.html).

This is where we are heading. About a month before the course, we planted a new Chinese parallel congregation out of the bilingual congregation. Our leadership team is now working hard to help each congregation intentionally love the other. Our growth strategy for the next ten years is to develop the multi-ethnic congregation *and* to plant at least one homogeneous congregation or fellowship each year.

Under God, we have already exceeded our initial target for the Chinese parallel congregation. My prayer is that as the Lord continues to add to our number those who are being saved, many more will glorify his name in their own heart language, among people of their own culture — and among people who are not like them at all. My prayer is that as an immigrant or a local walks into our church, "The secrets of his heart will be revealed, and as a result he will fall facedown and worship God, proclaiming, 'God is really among you'" (1 Corinthians 14:25).
