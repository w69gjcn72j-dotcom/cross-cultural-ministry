Being the Church in a Multi-Ethnic Community

Summary Why the multi-ethnic congregation and the monocultural congregation are both legitimate, why a church has to decide what kind of neighbour it is, and what it costs to plant a parallel congregation out of a bilingual one.

There is a version of the multi-ethnic church conversation that goes badly, and it goes badly in a predictable way. One side treats the multi-ethnic congregation as the only faithful model, and everything else as a failure of nerve. The other treats the language-specific congregation as the only realistic model, and everything else as a middle-class fantasy. Both sides can produce a congregation that is not flourishing to prove the point.

Gary McIntosh and Alan McMahan's Being the Church in a Multi-Ethnic Community was very helpful to me here, because it refuses that argument. They affirm that the multi-ethnic model is a good model — and that so is the monocultural one. The principle that governs both is that neither may be exclusive of the other. Room has to be made for both.

That is not a compromise. It is a recognition that these are answers to different questions.

Getting the words right first

Part of the trouble is that we use one word for several things. The course pressed on definitions early (McIntosh and McMahan 2012, 24), and the distinctions turn out to matter: multiracial, multinational, multiethnic, multilingual and multicultural are overlapping circles, not synonyms.

A congregation can be multiracial and culturally uniform. It can be monoethnic and deeply multicultural — as it is when first-generation, 1.5-generation and second-generation members of the same ethnicity gather, each carrying a genuinely different culture. It can be multilingual without being multicultural, if one language group is simply being accommodated inside another group's church.

Being precise about which of these we are actually talking about dissolves a surprising number of arguments before they start.

What kind of neighbour are we?

The framework from McIntosh and McMahan I have found most useful is their account of the postures a church can take towards the community around it: renter, investor, neighbour, co-worker, sibling (McIntosh and McMahan 2012, 31).

The value of the list is diagnostic. It is possible to have a full programme of community activity and still be a renter — present on the site, using the building, essentially unattached to the place. It is possible to be an investor, putting real resources into the community while keeping a careful distance from it. Neighbour, co-worker and sibling each cost more, and each require the community to have some claim on us rather than only the reverse.

I would want to ask of my own church not what our programmes are, but which of those five words an honest observer from the local council would use about us — and whether the answer has changed in eight years.

The homogeneous unit question, without the shouting

Behind all of this sits the long argument about the homogeneous unit principle: whether people are most readily reached and gathered among those culturally like themselves, and whether a church should build on that fact or resist it.

The course set that debate alongside a number of models for how congregations of different languages actually share a life — from Benjamin Shin's typology of residence models, to DJ Chuang's simpler and very serviceable distinction between parental, parallel and partnership relationships between congregations (Chuang, cited in course materials, 41), to Mark DeYmaz's principles for the intentionally multi-ethnic church.

The parental relationship is the one most immigrant churches begin with and the one most of them outgrow badly. One congregation holds the property, the budget and the decisions; the other is welcome, valued, and structurally a dependent. It works while the dependency is real and curdles the moment it is not.

The tension that Greg Haroutunian named in class is the one I feel most: ethnic mission against geographic and relational parish community. An Anglican parish has a geography. It has a boundary drawn on a map and a duty to the people inside it, whoever they turn out to be. An ethnic ministry has a people, and that people is scattered across many parishes. Both are legitimate. They pull in different directions every time you plan anything, and the pull does not resolve — it has to be managed.

What it has actually cost us

About a month before the course, we planted a new Chinese parallel congregation out of our bilingual congregation.

I want to be careful how I describe this, because plants are usually reported after the fact as strategy, and at the time they are mostly nerve. We had come out of lockdown with our resources stretched, had folded everything into one bilingual gathering, had grown to the point of reaching the building's capacity, and then divided that gathering in two. Under God, we have already exceeded our initial target for the new congregation. That is a mercy, not a vindication.

The cost is that two congregations who used to be in one room now have to choose to love each other. Nothing about parallel congregations produces that automatically; the structure makes it optional in a way that sitting in the same service did not. Our leadership team is now working hard at exactly this — helping each congregation intentionally love the other.

Our strategy for the next ten years is to hold both together: develop the multi-ethnic congregation, and plant at least one homogeneous congregation or fellowship each year. Not one model at the expense of the other. Both, on purpose.

Why bother with the harder arrangement

Because of what it is for.

My prayer is that as the Lord continues to add to our number those who are being saved, many more will glorify his name in their own heart language, among people of their own culture — and also among people not remotely like them. Those two are not in competition. The first is how most people come to understand the gospel at all. The second is how the world finds out what the gospel has done.

The test Paul gives is not whether the arrangement is elegant. It is whether an outsider walking in finds himself uncovered: "The secrets of his heart will be revealed, and as a result he will fall facedown and worship God, proclaiming, 'God is really among you'" (1 Corinthians 14:25).

That is the only reason any of this is worth the difficulty.
