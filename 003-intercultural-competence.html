Intercultural Competence

Summary Michael Chu's four factors of cultural intelligence, the three conflicts they explain, and why a minister's cultural illiteracy is not a personality quirk but a pastoral problem.

Before the course began I was asked to read Michael Chu's Intercultural Competence — Cultural Intelligence, Pastoral Leadership and The Chinese Church. It is a book written about my own city, about churches I know, and it named things I had felt for years without having language for.

Chu observes that immigrant ministry in Sydney is marked by many "unexpected disputes and setbacks" (Chu 2019, xx). The word that matters there is unexpected. These are not the ordinary frictions a minister braces for. They arrive from an angle nobody was watching, and they leave everyone baffled as well as wounded. Chu's argument is that a good number of them stop being mysterious once you look at them through cultural intelligence.

The four factors

Chu works with four factors of cultural intelligence, or CQ (Chu 2019, 143–144).

Metacognitive CQ — CQ Strategy. The capability for conscious intercultural awareness. Not knowing about a culture, but knowing that you are in one, and that the other person is in another, while it is happening.

Cognitive CQ — CQ Knowledge. Actual knowledge of intercultural norms, practices and conventions. What is meant by silence. What a gift obliges. Who speaks first.

Motivational CQ — CQ Drive. The capability to direct appropriate attention, drive, effort and energy towards learning about and functioning in diverse cultural contexts. This is the one nobody can do for you.

Behavioural CQ — CQ Action. The capability of displaying appropriate verbal and non-verbal action in intercultural interaction. Knowing is not enough; the body has to learn too.

It is worth noticing what kind of list this is. Only one of the four is knowledge. The other three are awareness, desire, and practice. A minister can read every book about a culture and still have almost no cultural intelligence, because he never once thought to ask whether his own way of running a meeting was cultural rather than obvious.

Three conflicts these factors explain

Chu identifies at least three points of tension in immigrant churches that come clear when read this way.

Intergenerational conflict arising from a pastor's cultural illiteracy

Some pastors prefer non-verbal communication; others, often those with more Western formation, prefer verbal communication. Both sides must acknowledge the need to widen their range (Chu 2019, 214–215).

This sounds like a small thing until you watch it happen. A first-generation pastor who has shown his care by turning up, by feeding people, by simply being present for twenty years, is told he never says anything encouraging. A second-generation pastor who has said, repeatedly and warmly, exactly what he thinks, is told he is cold. Each has communicated love in his own register and each has been heard as withholding it. Neither is lying about what he experienced.

Language deficiency and CQ Knowledge

Chu notes that even where senior Chinese pastors spend most of their ministry and personal life in Chinese, it is vital that they learn English — for the sake of mission, collaboration and evaluation (Chu 2019, 222).

The sharp edge of his argument is the framing. Learning the other language is not a professional competency. It is an act of love, undertaken to reduce the risk of conflict on a pastoral team. And it must run both ways: English-speaking pastors learning Chinese out of love for their Chinese-speaking colleagues.

I want to press this, because in my experience the obligation is almost never felt symmetrically. It is assumed that the Chinese-speaking pastor will make himself understood in English, and considered charming if the English-speaking pastor learns to say hello. That asymmetry teaches something. It teaches whose comfort is the default and whose effort is the courtesy.

Insufficient reflection and metacognitive CQ

Chu observes that many senior Chinese pastors are not especially self-reflective. Many are highly competent, but reactive — responding to the problem in front of them rather than observing trends and contemplating repeated patterns (Chu 2019, 227–228).

He is not accusing them of laziness. A pastor who has spent thirty years absorbing crises has been rewarded, every single time, for reacting well. Reflection has never once been urgent.

But both Chinese and English pastors need deep reflection to navigate intergenerational conflict, where the party who mainly expresses emotion non-verbally must think carefully about how he is sending and receiving messages from the party who mainly expresses it in words (Chu 2019, 231). That is metacognitive work. There is no shortcut through it, and it does not happen in the middle of an argument.

Why this is a pastoral matter, not a management one

It would be possible to read all this as a set of professional skills — useful, improvable, faintly corporate. I think that would be a mistake.

In a city where 37.4% of residents speak a language other than English at home, cultural intelligence is not an optional refinement for ministers. It is the ordinary equipment for loving your neighbour. And the command to love is not satisfied by good intentions poorly aimed. Love that consistently lands as indifference has a problem that sincerity will not fix.

There is a missional argument here and an ecclesiological one, and I would not want to collapse either into the other. Missionally, we cannot reach people we systematically misread. Ecclesiologically, the church is already the place where "there is not Greek and Jew, circumcision and uncircumcision, barbarian, Scythian, slave and free; but Christ is all and in all" (Colossians 3:11) — so a congregation that cannot hold two cultures together is failing to be what it already is in Christ.

Chu's observations of the immigrant churches in my own context have been very insightful, and a great many of them match what I have seen. It is worth saying that although the data on cultural intelligence among Chinese churches in Sydney is thin, it is still far more extensive than what exists for other immigrant churches here. Nepalese, Indian, Arabic-speaking and African congregations across this city are working through the same questions with almost nothing written for them.

It would benefit every church in Sydney if more were invested in the cultural intelligence of our leaders — as a missional incentive, and as an ecclesiological imperative.
